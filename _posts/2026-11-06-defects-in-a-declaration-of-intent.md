**A declaration of intent (oświadczenie woli) constitutes one of the cornerstones of civil law,**
**particularly in the context of contracts. Such a declaration is not, however, always legally effective.**
**The Civil Code sets out a number of situations in which the person making a declaration of intent**
**is entitled to avoid its legal consequences.**

## What is a Declaration of Intent

The substantial majority of civil-law relationships arise as a consequence of legal acts performed by persons with the legal capacity to carry them out. The typical means by which a legal act is performed is the making of a declaration of intent. In accordance with Art. 60 of the Civil Code, such a declaration may take any form (consisting of any conduct whatsoever), provided that it is sufficiently intelligible to the recipient.

A declaration of intent therefore encompasses not only a written document or an oral expression of willingness to conclude a contract, but also, for example, placing goods on a shop counter — it becomes clear to the seller that one wishes to purchase the item (i.e. one is making a declaration of intent with the aim of concluding a contract of sale).

The vast majority of people make dozens, if not hundreds, of declarations of intent every day. The overwhelming majority of these are valid and effective — there are, however, exceptions to this rule.

## Defects in a Declaration of Intent

The Civil Code identifies several typical situations in which a declaration of intent must be regarded as defective. These are (in accordance with Arts. 83–87 of the Civil Code):
- simulation (pozorność);
- mistake (błąd);
- distortion by a messenger (zniekształcenie przez posłańca);
- fraud (podstęp);
- duress (groźba).

Addressing each in turn:

1. Simulation occurs where a person makes a declaration of intent without, however, intending to bring about the legal effect that would ordinarily follow. An example might be feigning a gift of property to another person whilst in reality intending to sell it — the simulated gift being designed to achieve a tax advantage.
2. Mistake is a situation in which a person makes a declaration of intent whilst labouring under a misapprehension as to the factual or legal position. It frequently arises in employment matters — a mutual agreement to terminate an employment contract concluded by an employee who is unaware that she is pregnant is a classic instance of mistake.
3. Where a declaration of intent is made through a third party who — whether deliberately or inadvertently — distorts it, the declaration is defective (in a manner analogous to mistake). If one asks someone to propose on one's behalf the purchase of goods for one hundred złoty, and that person mishears and proposes one hundred thousand złoty, one is entitled to avoid the consequences arising from that error.
4. Fraud is a particular species of mistake, induced deliberately by the other party to the legal act.
5. Duress occurs where a declaration of intent is made out of fear of possible consequences — constituting a serious threat — to the personal safety or property of the declarant or of a person close to them.

## Avoiding the Consequences of a Defective Declaration

In the case of declarations made under the influence of mistake (including one induced by a messenger or by fraud) and of duress, the person who made such a declaration may avoid its legal consequences. In order to do so, the declarant must submit to the recipient a fresh declaration of intent (in writing) expressing the wish to avoid the consequences of the original declaration. The declarant has one year in which to do so — running from the discovery of the mistake or from the moment at which the state of fear induced by the duress ceased. The submission of a declaration avoiding the consequences of a declaration of intent removes that original declaration from legal circulation entirely — the position reverts to what it would have been had no such declaration ever been made.
