**Suretyship is one of those terms whose colloquial meaning is, at least broadly,**
**familiar to most of us. As is often the case, however, in legal language and legal**
**practice it carries a very precise meaning. What, then, is suretyship?**

## Suretyship under the Civil Code

Matters relating to suretyship are governed by Title XXXII of Book Three of the Civil Code, i.e. Arts. 876–887. Those provisions establish, above all, that suretyship is a contract — it is therefore founded on the concurring intention of at least two parties whose aim is to create a specific obligational relationship.

By virtue of concluding this contract, the surety (poręczyciel) undertakes towards the creditor to perform a specified obligation in the event that the debtor (the principal debtor) fails to do so. It is therefore necessary that a principal obligation (between the creditor and the debtor) already exists, the performance of which the surety agrees to secure. There is, however, an exception to this rule: it is possible to provide suretyship for a future debt (up to a pre-determined amount).

## Liability of the surety

The surety's obligation is of an accessory nature and is strictly dependent both on the principal obligation itself and on the conduct of the debtor. From a strictly legal perspective, the surety is, as a general rule, liable as a joint and several debtor (dłużnik solidarny), which means that the creditor may pursue payment from both of them simultaneously — and performance by the surety likewise discharges the debt of the principal debtor, and vice versa.

## The surety's position in the event of a dispute

Since the surety's duties are closely linked to those incumbent upon the debtor, the rights available to the surety — in particular with regard to the ability to defend his or her legal position — are analogous to those of the principal debtor. The surety may raise all defences available to the debtor (for example, invoking the limitation of the obligation, its extinguishment on objective grounds, and so forth), and may also set off a claim. Importantly, these rights are independent of those belonging to the principal debtor.
