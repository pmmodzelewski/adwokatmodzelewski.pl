**The provisions of the Family and Guardianship Code regulate the question of the level of maintenance**
**for minor children in a manner that is fundamentally open-ended and imprecise.**
**The extensive body of case law and legal doctrine developed in this area has given rise to**
**a number of principles, the foremost of which is the "equal standard of living" principle.**

## Provisions of the Family and Guardianship Code

Pursuant to Article 135 § 1 of the Family and Guardianship Code, the level of maintenance due
(applied also, and in practice primarily, in the context of minor children)
depends, firstly, on the justified needs of the child and, secondly, on the financial
capacity of the parent obliged to pay it.

This system is eminently sensible — by way of example, one may seek a different level of maintenance on behalf of a child who is healthy and developing normally than ought to be paid for a child requiring specialist medical treatment, care, or equipment. The needs of the entitled party are therefore of material significance.

At the same time, a lower level of maintenance should be imposed on a person who, despite their most genuine efforts, is unable to earn more than the minimum wage, while a higher level should be imposed on one whose monthly income runs to several hundred thousand złoty per month.

## The Equal Standard of Living Principle

The general guidance outlined above does not, however, yield a straightforward and concrete answer to the fundamental question — what level of maintenance is appropriate in a given situation. Assistance is provided by the equal standard of living principle developed through case law. Its essence is best summarised by the judgment of the Supreme Court of 5 May 1998, sygn. akt I CKN 284/98, according to which "the scope of a child's justified needs should be determined in such a way that, once those needs are met, the child's standard of living is the same as that of the parents. (...) the concern here is not with a mechanical division of the parents' income, but with satisfying the children's justified needs at a level comparable to that of the parents, having regard to their age, state of health, educational direction, and other individual circumstances."

## Application in Practice

In practice, this means that when establishing the baseline level of a child's justified needs, one must do so by reference to the parents' standard of living — in particular that of the parent who is required to pay maintenance. If that parent lives lavishly, is able, for example, to travel abroad regularly, resides in highly comfortable circumstances, and travels everywhere by private car, it must be assumed that the child too should have access to funds that make this possible.

The principle also operates in the other direction — if the financial situation of the obliged parent permits only a modest lifestyle, without comforts, luxuries, or unnecessary expenditure, the minor child cannot demand that that parent meet needs of a more far-reaching nature.
