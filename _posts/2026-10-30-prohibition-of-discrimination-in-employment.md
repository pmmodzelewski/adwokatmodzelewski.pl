**A key (and widely recognised) principle of the Labour Code is the prohibition of discrimination.**
**But what actually constitutes discrimination? How does this category differ from mere unequal treatment?**

## The Prohibition of Discrimination

Of fundamental importance in this context is Art. 11(3) of the Labour Code. It provides that any discrimination in employment is impermissible. The provision further specifies that neither direct nor indirect discrimination is permitted, and sets out in detail the particular grounds of discrimination that are taken into account.

Discrimination on the following grounds is therefore prohibited:
- sex;
- age;
- disability;
- race;
- religion;
- nationality;
- political beliefs;
- trade union membership;
- ethnic origin;
- denomination;
- sexual orientation;
- form of employment (fixed-term or indefinite contract, and full-time or part-time work).

## What Constitutes Discrimination?

The legislature develops these matters in Chapter IIa of the Labour Code, from Art. 18(3a) onwards. It follows from those provisions that employees should be treated equally regardless of the criteria set out above. Equal treatment means non-discrimination — which unfortunately complicates interpretation, as it requires us to turn to the definitions of further terms: direct and indirect discrimination. The legislature explains, however, that the former refers to a situation in which a person is treated worse (less favourably) on the basis of the specified criteria, whilst the latter refers to a situation in which the rules applied do not directly engage those criteria, but in their consequence a disproportionately large number of persons belonging to one of the protected groups would be treated less favourably.

## What Does Not Constitute Discrimination?

In order to understand correctly what discrimination is (and what it is not), it is necessary to recognise that not every situation in which two persons are not treated equally is unlawful. This may appear self-evident, yet it is worth noting that unequal treatment occurs in every workplace on a daily basis — a person employed in a more senior position may earn more than one employed in a more junior role, whilst a difference between persons working in the same positions may stem from experience or particular competencies. It must therefore be appreciated that unequal treatment constitutes discrimination (and as such breaches the provisions of the Labour Code) only where it is based on irrelevant criteria — that is, criteria which do not justify different treatment from a substantive point of view. The list of such criteria has been set out above.
