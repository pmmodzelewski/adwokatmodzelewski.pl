**The Labour Code provides for three ways of terminating an employment contract — by mutual agreement,**
**by notice, and without notice. The so-called "summary dismissal" falls within that third category.**

## Termination of an Employment Contract Without Notice

It should be noted at the outset that termination of an employment contract without notice does not always constitute a summary dismissal. Nor is it always connected with the employee's own conduct. Under the Labour Code, terminations without notice are divided into two categories: those without employee fault (in cases of prolonged incapacity for work due to illness or for other reasons — the distinction relating to the length of the absence) and those with employee fault.

## Employee Fault

Moreover, not every termination of an employment contract due to employee fault constitutes a "summary dismissal". The Labour Code identifies three situations in which a contract may be terminated in this manner:
- a serious breach of fundamental employment duties (the so-called summary dismissal);
- commission of an offence that precludes the continued employment of the employee (a final and binding judgment is required);
- culpable loss of the requisite qualifications or licences.

The second and third situations are, of course, considerably rarer in practice; it should nonetheless be borne in mind that termination without notice may arise, for example, where a professional driver loses their entitlement to drive vehicles (in connection with a breach of art. 178a of the Kodeks karny).

## Serious Breach of Fundamental Duties

From the perspective of this article — and indeed of practice, given how frequently it arises — the most significant ground is termination of an employment contract without notice due to employee fault on account of a serious breach by the employee of their fundamental employment duties. Crucially, each element of that formulation carries material weight both for the procedure of dismissal and for any subsequent proceedings brought by the employee before the Court.

First, the breach must be serious — that is to say, grave, and productive of deep and adverse consequences (or at least carrying the potential to produce such consequences). It is also important to note that the subjective element is taken into account, namely the employee's mental attitude towards the situation. For a breach to be characterised as serious, the employee must have acted deliberately, with profound disregard for the applicable rules, or indeed with the express intention of violating them.

At the same time, the breach must relate to fundamental employment duties. Even a breach that is serious and culpable, but which concerns matters that do not constitute the employee's core obligations, will not justify a summary dismissal.
